Shoalcare presents

The early-warning, lab and insurance network for farms that share water

Built in Ghana for fish farmers everywhere. ShoalWatch turns every farm on a lake, river or canal into part of one connected defence. A problem on one farm becomes a warning for its neighbours, a lab sample, and a data point that can unlock fair insurance and credit.

The problem

Farms on the same water get sick together, yet most farm tools see one farm at a time. Outbreaks are reported late, causes stay unconfirmed, and insurers and lenders cannot price a risk nobody measures. So farmers carry the whole loss alone.

Why Ghana, and why it can travel

Ghana's tilapia farms sit on shared waters such as the Volta system, where one farm's problem is every farm's problem. The same pattern holds on lakes, rivers and canals across Africa and Asia, so what is proven here can scale to other species and countries.

How Shoalcare earns

  • Farmer and association plans: advanced alerts, Analytica and reports for farms, cooperatives and fisheries officers.
  • Insurance and credit partners: a fee for each group policy or input loan made possible by verified farm data.
  • Labs and programmes: sampling networks and donor or government disease-surveillance contracts.
  • Consented research data: anonymised group-level insight, only with farmer agreement.

Why it is hard to copy

Every farm that joins makes the warnings better for its neighbours. Every confirmed lab result makes the data more valuable to insurers. Farmers own their records, which builds the trust that data networks depend on.

Principles we build on
  • Offline first. Low data use, built for places with weak signal.
  • Group alerts, not name and shame. Neighbours see that their water has abnormal mortality, not which farm.
  • Farmer data sovereignty. Records belong to the farmer, who can export (Download CSV) or delete them (Clear all) at any time. Each use, such as research, alerts or insurance, needs its own consent.
  • Transparent methods. Hotspots use a published screening rule and an optional false-discovery correction, so alerts stay trustworthy.
  • Honest wording. Everything here is suspected disease-related mortality. Low oxygen, poor water quality and rough handling also kill fish, and only laboratory tests confirm a disease.

Site selection advice

Pin the planned site (tap the map, use GPS, or search a place), then compare it with the farms you have logged.

Site checklist

Pond sites
  • Choose soil that holds water: clay or clay loam. Avoid sandy, gravelly or very porous soil.
  • Use gently sloping land so you can fill and drain by gravity. Give each pond its own inlet and outlet so water never runs from one pond to the next.
  • Stay above the highest flood mark. Floods bring wild fish, pathogens and pollution.
  • Pond depth is commonly about 1 to 1.5 m, with sloping banks.
  • Leave room for a settling pond, an access road, a harvest area and a disposal pit for dead fish well away from water.
Cage sites
  • Pick open, well-flushed water with a steady current. Avoid stagnant bays, dead-end arms and very shallow areas.
  • Keep clear water below the cage floor (commonly at least 2 m) so waste disperses and oxygen stays up.
  • Keep cage groups well apart from each other and from neighbouring farms, and do not site cages directly downstream of another farm. Agree zoning with neighbours and the local fisheries authority.
  • Avoid ferry routes, fishing grounds and river mouths that carry silt or pollution. Check wind and wave exposure before anchoring.
Water source and surroundings
  • Test dissolved oxygen, temperature, pH, turbidity and ammonia before you build, and again in the dry and rainy seasons.
  • Avoid water downstream of mining (including small-scale illegal mining), quarries, factories, markets, sewage or heavily sprayed farmland. Silt, heavy metals and agrochemicals can harm fish and consumers.
  • Prefer your own spring, borehole or stream, or a position upstream of other farms. If water must be shared, agree biosecurity with every farm on it first.
  • Confirm permits and land or water-use rights with the Fisheries Commission and the district assembly before you build.
  • Check road access, feed and fingerling supply, power, security and a market or cold chain for harvest.

Biosecurity advice

Before stocking
  • Buy fingerlings from a known hatchery that can show health records. Ask whether fingerlings or broodstock have been tested for TiLV where testing is available.
  • Quarantine new fish separately for at least 2 weeks and watch them before they join existing stock.
  • Dry and clean empty ponds in the sun, repair dykes, and screen inlets to keep wild fish out.
  • Stock all-in, all-out for each pond or cage group. Do not add new batches to fish already growing.
  • Follow recommended stocking densities. Overcrowding is a major stress and disease trigger.
During production
  • Check fish and water every day. Measure oxygen early in the morning, when it is lowest. Watch for gasping, spinning, lying at the surface, skin or gill lesions, pop-eye and swollen bellies.
  • Remove dead and dying fish every day with a dedicated net or bucket, and bury them with lime or compost well away from water. Never throw dead fish into a pond, river or canal.
  • Do not share nets, buckets, boots or scales between ponds, cages or farms unless they are cleaned, disinfected and dried in the sun.
  • Use a footbath and hand washing at the entrance, limit visitors and fish traders, and keep dogs and poultry away from ponds.
  • Feed fresh, dry feed stored off the floor. Do not overfeed, and cut feeding when fish look unwell or oxygen is low.
  • Keep records of stocking, feeding, daily deaths, water readings, treatments and visitors. Good records make this app far more useful.
  • Use antibiotics or chemicals only on the advice of a veterinarian or fisheries officer, and follow withdrawal periods before harvest.
When deaths start
  • Act the same day. Record the count, time and signs, and take photos.
  • Check oxygen and water quality. Add aeration or water flow if oxygen is low, but only with clean inflow water.
  • Reduce or stop feeding for the day, and do not move fish, nets or water to other ponds.
  • Call your district fisheries officer, the Veterinary Services Directorate, your hatchery or a veterinarian. Keep a few fresh, sick or just-dead fish cold on ice in a sealed container for laboratory testing, and do not add preservatives unless the laboratory tells you to.
  • Warn neighbours who share your water so they can check their fish. Do not release water from affected ponds until you have advice.
  • After harvest or loss, clean, disinfect, dry and rest the pond before restocking.
Farms that share water
  • Agree to tell each other the same day when abnormal deaths start.
  • Do not discharge water from sick stock into a shared channel or water body.
  • Synchronise stocking and fallow periods where you can, and keep a shared contact list.
  • Log every event in this app with the same water source name so clusters show up on the map.
